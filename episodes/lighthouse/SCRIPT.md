# The Man Who Wished for the Storm: script v1 (442 words, ~143 s at the creator's 1.10x pace)

Hook A (65 WORKABLE, The Superlative).

You'd never wish for the worst storm in history, but in 1703 one man did, and nobody saw him again.
It all starts with a rock. Fourteen miles off the coast of England, a reef so low the sea swallows it twice a day. For years it had been tearing open ships in the dark.
One man had lost two of his own ships to it, so he decided to do something nobody had ever tried. Build a lighthouse on it. With his own money.
His name was Henry Winstanley. An engraver, an inventor, a showman who ran a theatre of water tricks in London. Not an engineer.
In the summer of 1696, he and his workers start rowing out from Plymouth. They can only work at low tide, in calm weather, and the waves keep washing their work away.
That entire first summer, all they manage is twelve holes in the rock.
Then it gets worse. England is at war with France, and in 1697 a French privateer sails up, smashes the work, and drags Winstanley off to France as a prisoner.
The story goes that King Louis the Fourteenth heard about it and let him go, saying France was at war with England, not with humanity.
So he goes straight back to his rock.
And in November 1698, the light finally shines. Candles, on a wooden tower, in the middle of the sea.
That first winter, the waves climb right over the top and bury the lantern.
So he wraps the whole tower inside a bigger one. Taller, thicker, and because he's a showman, covered in carvings, ironwork and an open balcony. A palace on a rock.
For five years, it works. Ships finally have a light to steer by.
But people keep warning him. One day, the sea will take it.
And his answer became famous. He said he only wished he could be inside it during the greatest storm that ever blew, just to see what it would do.
In November 1703, he rows out to repair it, with his workers and the keepers.
And on the night of the 26th, he gets his wish. The worst storm ever recorded in Britain tears across the country. Thirteen navy warships go down. Thousands of people die.
The next morning, people on the shore look out to sea. Through the telescope, there is nothing there.
No tower. No light. Just a few bent iron bars sticking out of the rock. Henry Winstanley and the men inside were never found.
And just days later, a ship sailing home in the dark hit that same rock, because there was no light anymore.
