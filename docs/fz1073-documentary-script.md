## 0:44 Chapter 1: The flight

**VO:**
Flight FZ1073 took off from Dubai at 3:05 in the morning, UTC. The aircraft was a Boeing 737 MAX 8, registration A6-FKF.

Saudi aviation authorities later put the number on board at 174 passengers and eight crew. Israeli outlet Ynet reported that 27 of the passengers were children.

In the left seat was the captain, Smit Machchhar. He's Indian. Before Flydubai he spent eleven years at SpiceJet, where he rose to senior commander, and he'd been a captain at Flydubai since June 2022. Reports put his time on the 737 at more than nine thousand seven hundred hours. CNN's profile of him calls him jovial, and a father.

In the right seat was the first officer. His name is Hamam al-Hammami.

## 1:36 Chapter 2: The man in the right seat

**VO:**
Al-Hammami is 29 and an Omani national. According to reports, he was born in the UAE to a Syrian mother and spent several years in the Emirates.

In 2023 he finished a three-year distance-learning degree in aviation management from Buckinghamshire New University in the UK.

Then came Oman Air. In 2024, while he was a trainee co-pilot there, he was grounded. Reports say investigators found extremist material in his possession. He wasn't fired. Oman Air kept him on in an administrative role.

Later that year, authorities believe, he moved to Dubai. And according to reporting by Fox News, Flydubai did not check with Oman Air before hiring him.

That gap is still the biggest open question in this story. A Gulf diplomat told the Associated Press he went through standard security checks. What isn't clear is whether Oman's concerns were ever shared with another airline or another government. Israeli media have asked whether Israel's own security service vetted the crew of a flight landing in Tel Aviv at all.

So on the morning of September 30th, a man grounded by one airline for extremist material was sitting at the controls of another airline's jet, heading to Israel.

**ON SCREEN:** A dossier-style timeline: 2023 degree, 2024 Oman Air grounding, 2024 move to
Dubai, 2026 FZ1073. Each claim has its source name in small type under it. On "standard security
checks", a checklist appears with a question mark over the box "Oman Air consulted?".

## 2:59 Chapter 3: 05:21

**VO:**
For two hours, nothing looked wrong. The jet climbed to about 34,000 feet and crossed Saudi Arabia, heading northwest toward the Jordanian border.

At 5:21 UTC, Flightradar24's data shows the altitude starting to fluctuate.

Inside the cockpit, Captain Machchhar's first thought was mechanical. He has said he thought something catastrophic had happened to the aircraft. Then he understood. It wasn't the aircraft. It was the other man in the cockpit.

The UAE's Prosecutor General, Hamad Saif Al Shamsi, later described what happened next. The co-pilot, he said, attacked the captain on the flight deck using a crash axe, and tried to take control of the aircraft.

Every airliner carries a crash axe in the cockpit. Regulators require it. It's there so the crew can break open a panel to reach a hidden fire, or cut their way out after a crash. It's a safety tool that sits within reach of the pilots, which is exactly where it has to be.

At 5:22, the plane fell. Flight-tracking data shows FZ1073 dropping more than fourteen thousand feet in under thirty seconds. Other reports put the total loss closer to seventeen thousand feet.

In the cabin, nobody knew what was happening up front. One passenger later told i24NEWS, "We didn't know if the pilot was alive."

## 4:28 Chapter 4: The door

**VO:**
After 9/11, cockpit doors were rebuilt to stop anyone forcing their way in. Crew outside can request entry with a code. The pilot inside decides whether it opens.

On FZ1073, that design was working against everyone on board. According to the captain's account, crew members outside tried to get in using the access code, and the co-pilot kept blocking them. There was no way to open that door from the cabin side.

The captain was on the floor. He's described being so badly wounded that he'd gone down and wasn't sure he'd get back up. Speaking later to India's Prime Minister, Narendra Modi, he said this:

"When I was down, I could feel that the aircraft was plummeting, and I knew this is a last chance to do something. I knew the door was just there."

The door opened with a knob on the inside. He described it as a short, quick twist.

"Once I had my hands on the knob, I twisted and opened it."

That's the first of the four things that had to go right. The one man who could open the door from inside was still conscious, and he'd fallen within reach of it.

**ON SCREEN:** Diagram of the door from above: cabin side with a keypad marked "ACCESS CODE:
BLOCKED", cockpit side with the knob. A figure on the floor beside it. The captain's quote set
over slow, dark cockpit footage, word by word. Box 1 fills in: "THE CAPTAIN OPENED THE DOOR".

## 5:52 Chapter 5: The people who ran in

**VO:**
The first people through were passengers.

Yaniv Hayun was one of them. He's said that getting in was hard because the captain's body was lying against the door. He got hold of the co-pilot, pulled him off the controls in a chokehold and dragged him out of the cockpit.

Asaf Rajuan went in with him and helped overpower and hold the attacker down. Dr. Shota Musayev helped restrain him, then turned to the captain, who was badly hurt.

Two more people on that plane mattered more than anyone knew at boarding. Two airline pilots were flying as passengers, off duty. The industry calls it deadheading. Reports describe one as South African and one from New Zealand. Passengers knew one of them only as James.

They went into the cockpit with the cabin crew and the passengers. Hayun has said he went back in and helped pull back on the controls to level the aircraft. The off-duty pilots took over.

The co-pilot was tied up with headphone cables at first, and later with zip ties. Passengers say he shouted, "Kill me, kill me."

That's the second and third things. Passengers who ran toward the danger instead of away from it. And two qualified pilots, sitting in the cabin by chance, on the one flight that needed them.

## 7:22 Chapter 6: 7500

**VO:**
On the ground, controllers could only see numbers.

At 5:28 UTC the transponder switched to 7700, the general emergency code. About six minutes later it changed to 7500. That code has only one meaning: unlawful interference. A hijacking.

Israeli media reported that the Israeli Air Force scrambled fighter jets within minutes. But nothing outside that aircraft could help the people inside it.

The plane briefly crossed into Jordanian airspace, then made a full turn back and headed southwest, toward Tabuk in northwestern Saudi Arabia. It held at about fifteen thousand feet. As it approached Tabuk, the code went back to 7700.

At 9:45 in the morning local time, FZ1073 landed at Prince Sultan bin Abdulaziz Airport.

That's the fourth thing. With the cockpit secured and pilots at the controls, everyone on board landed alive.

The captain was taken to hospital in Tabuk with serious stab wounds. By the next morning he was stable enough to be flown to Abu Dhabi. The passengers were put on a replacement aircraft and finished their journey.

## 8:35 Chapter 7: The questions

**VO:**
Within days, it stopped being described as an altercation.

The aircraft is registered in the UAE, so under UAE law the case is theirs. On October 3rd, the UAE's Prosecutor General said the investigation found the co-pilot had attempted to carry out a terrorist attack. The suspect was transferred to the UAE.

Then came the claims about motive. US President Donald Trump, asked whether the co-pilot had links to Iran, said, "Based on what I'm hearing, yes, but we're working on it right now." He didn't give evidence. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said the co-pilot had undergone what he called Islamist radical indoctrination, but cautioned that it was too early to say whether he was linked to Iran or anyone else.

Flydubai suspended its flights to and from Israel while the investigation continues.

And the question from chapter two is still open. How did someone grounded for extremist material in 2024 pass the checks for a cockpit seat in 2026? Nobody has given a full answer yet.

## 9:46 Payoff

**VO:**
So here's the answer to the question at the start: how did a bleeding captain reach that doorknob?

He didn't have to reach far. The door was built so that only the person inside the cockpit could open it, and when he went down, he went down right next to it. The man trying to crash the plane could block every code from the cabin. He couldn't stop one twist from the floor.

That one twist worked because of what was on the other side: passengers who went straight into the cockpit, and two off-duty pilots who could fly the plane once they got there. Take away any one of those four things and this is a very different film.

this time it swings open.
